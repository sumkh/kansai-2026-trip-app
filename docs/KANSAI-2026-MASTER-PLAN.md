## 2. Read This Before You Pack

### There will be no autumn colours

Kyoto's foliage peaks **late November to early December**; Osaka and the wider Kansai region **mid to late November**. On your dates the maples are fully green and it will be **28–31°C and humid**. Pack for summer. Typhoon season is also still live — check that your travel insurance covers low-cost-carrier disruption specifically, not just full-service delays.

### You land on the harvest moon, but you are not chasing it

The National Astronomical Observatory of Japan confirms **中秋の名月 — Chūshū no
Meigetsu, the harvest moon — falls on Friday 25 September 2026**, with moonrise
in Kansai around **17:56**. The astronomical full moon is two days later on the
27th, which is normal.

**Decision taken 4 August: no moon-viewing event.** Daikaku-ji's Kangetsu no
Yūbe and the Shimogamo alternative are both out of the plan, and Friday evening
is a gym session instead. The moon still rises whether or not you book a boat —
if the eastern sky is clear after Nara, tsukimi dango from any wagashi shop and
a walk along the Kamogawa costs nothing.

### What else is seasonal on your dates

**Higanbana** (red spider lilies) bloom right at the equinox — vivid crimson along rice-paddy edges, notably around Asuka in Nara and Osawa Pond. **Hagi** (bush clover), one of the seven autumn flowers, peaks in September. **Susuki** (pampas grass) appears everywhere as tsukimi decoration. And **shokuyoku no aki**, the autumn of appetite: sanma, matsutake, chestnuts, new-harvest rice, figs, Kyoho grapes.

**You get the autumn moon, the autumn flowers and the autumn table — just not the trees.**

### One timing win

Japan's tax-free system changes on **1 November 2026** from an instant in-store 10% deduction to an airport refund model. Your trip is in September, so **you still get the deduction at the register**. Carry your passport when shopping.

## 6. Food Guide

### 6.1 Arima — the one that needs planning ⚠️

**No beef, and Arima closes early.** Because almost everyone eats inside their ryokan, most restaurants and shops in Arima shut early in the evening and the town's dining is weighted heavily toward lunch. Add that **21, 22 and 23 September are all national holidays** and small restaurants will keep irregular hours. This is the only place on the trip where dinner needs to be solved in advance rather than found on the night.

**Plan A — the no-beef kaiseki at Goshobo.** See Section 3.2(d). Their Akashi-Ura fish is the point of the place. Solving it this way removes all closing-time risk and is almost certainly the best food of the trip.

**Plan B — eating in town.** These are the realistic non-beef options:

| Where | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Soba Dosanjin (土山人)** · 1056 Arima-cho | **The pick.** 11:00–15:00 and 17:00–20:00, closed Wednesdays, 6 min from Arima Onsen Station. Signature **sudachi soba** — cold noodles under sliced sudachi citrus — normally with a queue. Soba and tempura, no beef required. **Accepts dinner reservations; book ahead.** Private room upstairs |
| **Aramiya** · izakaya nearest Kin no Yu | Where locals drink every evening. Fresh sashimi and tempura, plus onigiri, tai-chazuke, udon and soba, so it works as a full dinner. Casual, cheap, open late |
| **Arima 18-ban** | Shirasu (young sardines) over rice, and **akashiyaki** — the Hyogo specialty, soft egg-rich octopus dumplings dipped in dashi rather than sauce. Very local, no beef |
| **Taiko no Yu dining** | You're there all Tuesday anyway. The complex has restaurants and stays open to 22:00 — the most reliable evening option in Arima |
| ~~Gekkoen Yugetsusanso~~ | **Avoid.** Dinner is by complete reservation at 17:45 or 19:30, but explicitly not on public holidays — which rules out both your nights |

**Plan C — the Monday depachika trick.** The Hankyu highway bus terminal sits inside the Hankyu Sanbangai building at Umeda, and directly below it is one of Japan's great **depachika food halls**. Buy dinner there before boarding. Monday evening is the night most exposed to Arima's early closing, and depachika bento offer far more variety than anything you will find in Arima after dark. The 12:50 bus leaves you time to buy in the same building you depart from.

**Breakfast in Arima.** Skip hotel breakfast in Osaka and Kyoto without hesitation; Kuromon Ichiba and Nishiki Market are far better. **Arima is the exception** — take the ryokan breakfast at least on Wednesday, since you're out before 10:00 and nothing in town is open. A Japanese ryokan breakfast is grilled fish, rice, miso, pickles and tamago — no beef anywhere near it, and a highlight rather than a chore.

If you do skip it on Tuesday, two bakeries are worth the walk:
- **Pan du Bo** — opens 9:00, sells until sold out. Normally closed Tuesdays, **but closes the following day instead when Tuesday is a holiday**, so it will be open for you
- **Houkyuuan** — 10:00–18:00. Tanba black bean, Arima sansho and Kinsen salt breads, all of which sell out by mid-afternoon

- [ ] Cash in yen
- [ ] Tattoo cover if needed

**Etiquette:** never drop weights — taken far more seriously than in Singapore. Wipe machines down. Tattoos covered.

### One practical note

In 28–31°C humidity, on 15–20k steps a day, training on an empty stomach and then not eating will cost you the session before it costs you anything else. Japan makes this easy to avoid without a sit-down dinner: your Dormy Inn serves **free ramen every night**, depachika and konbini food is genuinely good, and standing noodle bars take ten minutes. Eat something around training — it needn't be a two-hour meal.
